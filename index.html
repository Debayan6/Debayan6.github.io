I. Echoes of the Cosmos: A Student's Exploration of Radio Astronomy

Hello there, dear readers! I'm a third-year BSMS student majoring in Physics at the prestigious Indian Institutes of Science Education and Research, Kolkata. Here, I have the privilege of diving deep into the wonders of our universe, deciphering its intriguing secrets through the language of science. Today, I invite you on a remarkable journey, a journey that encompasses the echoes of the cosmos, the invisible trails of radio astronomy, and the relentless pursuit of knowledge that defines my daily existence.

First things first, let me share with you the love of my academic life - Radio Astronomy. Imagine this discipline as a mystical telescope, not limited by the confines of visual light. With it, we can hear the cosmic whispers from the farthest corners of the universe, transcending space and time to witness the very first acts of cosmic theatre - the birth of galaxies, stars, and planets.

Now, you might wonder, why radio astronomy? Picture yourself standing on a beach at night, facing the vast ocean, with nothing but the sounds of waves reaching out to you. In a way, the universe is like this vast, dark ocean, and radio waves are these distant whispers - the echo of the waves - reaching out to us, carrying stories of unseen worlds and celestial phenomena. To me, radio astronomy is the dark sea navigator, guiding us through the cosmic ocean, letting us listen and understand these distant tales.

Currently, I'm engrossed in a fascinating project, whimsically titled "Search for OH megamaser in IR-selected objects." And no, it's not the name of a science fiction novel, but believe me, it's every bit as enthralling.

You see, an OH megamaser (OHMM) is a galaxy that emits enormous amounts of microwave radio signals, specifically from hydroxyl (OH) molecules. Picture these megamasers as cosmic lighthouses, illuminating the universe with their intense radio waves. We are currently utilizing data from the Meerkat Absorption Line Survey (MALS) to search for these elusive celestial objects, our detectors sensitive to their unique spectral fingerprint.

Supervising this research is none other than Prof. Neeraj Gupta, the esteemed Principal Investigator of the MALS. Under his guidance, we are not merely searching for these megamaser-emitting galaxies. We are attempting to unravel their properties, to understand their distribution in the cosmic fabric, and ultimately, to comprehend the processes that give rise to these incredible cosmic phenomena.

Away from the celestial whispers and galactic lighthouses, I find solace in a game of chess and the melodious strumming of my ukulele. Chess, with its intricate strategies and careful manoeuvres, mirrors the analytical rigour needed in physics. Each game, much like each research problem, is a puzzle waiting to be solved, a story waiting to be told. And as for the ukulele, its harmonious tunes are a gentle reminder of the celestial symphony played by the universe, filled with resonant vibrations, rhythms, and melodies.

In conclusion, my journey in physics and astronomy is much like a cosmic dance. Sometimes it's slow and methodical, like the patient search for OH megamasers. Other times it's dynamic and swift, like a game of chess or a melodious strum on the ukulele. But no matter the rhythm, it always strives to resonate with the vast cosmic symphony playing all around us. And as I navigate this path, the biggest joy lies in sharing these cosmic tales with you, inviting you to join in this grand dance of discovery.

So, I invite you, dear readers, to join me
